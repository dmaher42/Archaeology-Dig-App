# Standalone Game Rule

This document protects **Lost Site Expedition** from drifting into a worksheet-style, instruction-driven, or classroom-first experience.

The game must work first as a standalone indie archaeology adventure.

The player should want to play because the movement, exploration, danger, mystery, combat, bosses, upgrades, secrets, atmosphere, story, and discovery moments are satisfying on their own.

The Ancient Egypt grounding should be felt through the world and the player's actions. Players may learn about tombs, pharaohs, afterlife beliefs, preservation, memory, symbols, relics, and interpretation because those ideas shape the game world, not because the game stops to teach them.

---

## Core Rule

> Game first. Learning through the world.

Do not build disconnected educational layers that stop the player repeatedly for quiz questions, long explanations, worksheet-style tasks, or excessive exposition during core gameplay.

Historical grounding should make the adventure more believable and meaningful. It should not turn the game into a lesson delivery system.

---

## Correct Player Fantasy

The player fantasy is:

> I am an explorer caught inside a dangerous sacred site, trying to survive, understand what happened, and restore what was broken.

Not:

> I am completing a history worksheet inside a game.

---

## Design Model

Lost Site Expedition can be inspired by the way historically grounded games create immersion: research gives the world weight, but fiction drives the story.

For this project:

- Ancient Egypt is the inspiration and foundation.
- The Lost Site is fictional, mythic, and supernatural.
- Asha's story is original.
- Tomb objects, afterlife logic, pharaohs, guardians, murals, inscriptions, and relics should feel historically inspired without needing to be literal classroom explanations.
- The player should understand Ancient Egypt through gameplay, atmosphere, conflict, mystery, and consequence.

---

## Preferred Design Approach

Prefer:

- discovery through exploration
- archaeology-like verbs embedded into gameplay
- environmental storytelling
- murals and inscriptions
- ruins and architecture
- tomb layout that implies belief and ritual purpose
- relics that have story and mechanical meaning
- field notes as optional flavour, not mandatory reading
- puzzles based on evidence, symbols, placement, memory, or restoration
- cinematic discovery moments
- meaningful rewards tied to restoring the site
- optional deeper lore for players who want it

Avoid relying on:

- disconnected quiz popups
- long educational text walls during action gameplay
- constant pausing for explanation
- fake worksheet interfaces
- direct learning objectives inside the adventure
- overexplaining historical facts
- educational tasks that interrupt pacing

---

## Story Tone Rule

The game should not say, "Here is what Ancient Egyptians believed."

It should make the player feel:

- this tomb was built for a reason
- these objects are not simple treasure
- names, bodies, images, and offerings matter
- stealing an object can damage more than a room
- a false story can wound memory
- Anubis judges actions, not words
- the Queen's legacy may have been misunderstood

The player should learn by realising the world works differently from a treasure-hunter fantasy.

---

## Character Rule

Characters should feel like believable game characters first.

Asha should be a protagonist, not a lecture-delivery system. She is a present-day heritage researcher pulled into a sacred memory-world. She can observe, question, infer, react, fight, survive, and change through action.

Anubis should feel intimidating, ancient, intelligent, and judgement-focused. He is not a tutorial popup and should not become friendly too early.

The Queen should not be a simple evil boss. She is the centre of the contested interpretation: villain, protector, victim of false memory, or all three depending on what the player discovers.

Guardians should feel like protectors of a sacred system, not random monsters placed in the way.

---

## Excavation / Interpretation Rule

Excavation and interpretation can exist as reward layers after major discovery moments, but they must still feel like game systems.

They should contain:

- meaningful evidence decisions
- hidden finds
- reconstruction
- careful excavation
- evidence quality consequences
- interpretation choices
- route and reward logic
- story consequences

They should not become a static administration screen or classroom worksheet.

---

## Core Story Anchor

The game's most important historical-fantasy idea is:

> The past is not treasure to own. It is memory to protect.

Anubis' central reveal should sharpen this into:

> It was not treasure they stole. It was memory.

This line should not be used too early. It should land after the player has seen enough evidence that tomb objects, names, murals, bodies, records, and offerings are memory anchors.

---

## Core Design Goal

A player with no outside learning context should still want to play Lost Site Expedition as a standalone indie adventure.

If the game remains compelling without the educational context, the historical grounding becomes stronger rather than weaker.
